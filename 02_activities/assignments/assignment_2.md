The good example: https://www.behance.net/gallery/7171227/Bloom (Accessed from datavisproject.com). I classified this as a good visualization because it effectively balances aesthetics with functional clarity, while adhering to several key principles outlined in the course materials.

1. The Bloom visualization organizes planting times across different months into a clean, grid-like structure. By aligning plants visually with their respective active months radially in the plot, the design uses the Gestalt Principle of Proximity, where objects close together are perceived as a group. This allows users to instantly perceive the group of plants suitable for a specific blooming season without having to cross-reference multiple tables, lowering the cognitive load (extraneous load specifically) required to understand the data. 
2. This chart is clearly designed for home gardeners whose primary goal is planning a garden schedule. It avoids cluttering the viewer with scientific botanical jargon or metrics. Instead it uses a simple color-coded system to distinguish different types of plants (bulb, annual, and perennial), satisfying the substantive quality of accurately presenting data while maintaining the perceptual quality of being easily understood by the intended audience.
3. The Bloom visualization uses a harmonious color palette and clean typography, making it an attractive object that invites engagement. However, it remains factual and does not fall into the trap of prioritizing form over function. Unlike the 3D life expectancy example in the course material, the Bloom chart remains 2D, presenting data in a flat radial bar-chart style that accurately conveys the duration of planting windows. 

The bad example: https://food52.com/story/15618-what-it-means-to-reinvent-the-coffee-flavor-wheel?utm_source=Facebook&utm_medium=SocialMarketing&utm_campaign=Social (Accessed from datavizproject.com). I classified this as a bad visualization because it suffers from excessive complexity and accessibility issues that hinder comprehension.

1. The reinvented coffee flavor wheel presents a huge amount of data in a single view, with various tiers, color-coded sections, and specific technical lexicon, resulting in overwhelming intrinsic and extraneous cognitive load. By presenting hundreds of flavor descriptions simultaneously in a sunburst diagram, the visualization creates a high cognitive burden. It forces the users to rotate the text (or their head) to read the outer layers, which adds unnecessary extraneous load. This violates the principle of keeping composition concise.
2. It is important to consider the expertise of the intended audience. While this tool might be useful for coffee masters, for the general public, it is incomprehensible. It relies on specific germane knowledge that the average viewer lacks. Such flavor wheels would require extra training to use effectively, meaning the visualization fails to stand on its own as a communicative tool for a general viewer.
3. While the chart attempted to use the Gestalt principle of similarity by grouping flavors by color, the huge amount of distinct colors leads to a confusing diagram that is difficult to comprehend. The distinctions between adjacent shades can be subtle, leading to further accessibility issues for users with color vision impairments. Furthermore, the radial texts offer poor readability compared to linear list or table.

To improve the bad visualization, I suggest:

1. Employing an interactive interface. Instead of showing all three layers of the wheel at once, only present a simple, high-level view of the first layer. When the user wants to explore and click a specific category, it expands to reveal the sub-flavors. This makes the visualization to become an exploratory experience and reduces the initial cognitive load.
2. Breaking the wheel into linear groups. Breaking the wheel into separate distinct clusters for each major flavor profile follows the Gestalt Principle of Enclosure and Proximity. By separating the groups, the texts can also be more legible. This further reduces the extraneous load of reading radial texts.
